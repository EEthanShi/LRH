01Overview

Studies Apply Criteria at Different Points of One Lattice

The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) holds that increasingly capable models converge toward a common representation. However, recent studies have shown different views on aligning representations via their neighborhoods, spanning trees, and distances.

We show that these views differ because the alignment criteria behind them sit at different points of one lattice, and we formalize this explanation as the Lattice Representation Hypothesis (LRH).

Lattice Representation Hypothesis (LRH)

In the lattice of alignment criteria, the set of criteria accepting two models describes what is shared by their representations. Studies have conflicting conclusions because they apply criteria at different points of this lattice. For independently trained models, the training procedure and the objective decide the criteria met reliably across runs, each in a different part of the lattice.

An Increasing Link Leaves Every Neighbor Set Unchanged and Lowers CKA

Two representations can differ in many ways, and each criterion detects only some of these differences. In the animation, the squared distances of model B are those of model A passed through the increasing function \(g_t\), which links the limits of an InfoNCE learner and a spectral contrastive learner in Theorem 3.

Since \(g_t\) keeps the order of all distances, every neighbor set and the spanning tree stay unchanged, while centered kernel alignment (CKA) falls below one. Such a pair of models can satisfy a neighbor criterion and fail a CKA criterion through this link alone.

Main Findings

Under this lattice view, we obtain the following results.

  1. PRHThe convergence claimed by the PRH is agreement at the top of the lattice, whereas its evidence comes from neighbor overlap, a criterion near the bottom.
  2. ARHThe Aristotelian Representation Hypothesis (ARH) restricts the claimed convergence to local neighborhoods because calibration keeps the growth of neighbor overlap with model size but largely removes that of CKA. We show that this removal can come from calibrating a maximum over layers, so global convergence remains open. On LLaMA-1, calibration turned the size trend of CKA from 0.68 into −0.85, a drop predicted from single-model features, while fixed-depth calibrated CKA keeps its trend.
  3. BPCBack into Plato’s Cave (BPC) infers coarse shared structure from the collapse of neighbor overlap on larger evaluation sets, whereas we trace this collapse at least partly to a fixed number of neighbors covering shorter distances. As in BPC, neighbor overlap with sentence encoders (preregistered) and with last-layer vision features (exploratory) stays close to stable when \(k\) grows in proportion to the number of samples.
  4. SOGEvery minimum spanning tree (MST) contains the nearest-neighbor edge of each sample, and on the studied vision–language pairs these edges make up about 88% of the shared tree edges. The spanning-tree overlap reported by Structure over Geometry (SOG) thus mostly repeats one-nearest-neighbor overlap, while the number of other shared edges exceeds its chance level without an established size trend.
  5. TrainingThe training procedure of a linear autoencoder decides whether distances agree across runs by selecting the invertible map undone by its linear head. In a finite contrastive model, two learners trained with different contrastive objectives agree on the order of distances and differ in geometry, since they learn one relation through different increasing functions.
  6. ExperimentsExperiments on dozens of pretrained vision and language models and on thousands of runs of both theoretical models support these conclusions.

02The Lattice of Alignment Criteria

Neighbor Sets and Spanning Trees Lie on Two Branches below the Order of All Distances

What a Criterion Is

An alignment criterion is a test that compares how two models represent a shared set of evaluation samples, through one relation among these samples (e.g., the neighbor sets in mutual \(k\)-nearest-neighbor overlap (mKNN) or the distances in calibrated CKA). The criterion accepts the pair of models if the two relations agree within a tolerance under a fixed scale and protocol.

An observable maps the pairwise inner products of the samples in one model to such a relation, and one observable refines another if its value determines the value of the other.

Stronger and Weaker Criteria

We call one criterion stronger than another if every pair accepted by the first is accepted by the second, so one pair of models can satisfy a criterion and fail a stronger one. If we also count the criterion requiring agreement under both of two criteria (e.g., on both neighbor sets and spanning trees) and the criterion requiring agreement under either, the criteria form a lattice under strength.

For criteria with zero tolerance and a common protocol, strength reduces to refinement of the observables, and Proposition 1 of the paper describes this part of the lattice.

Two Branches

Just below all distances lies the observable of CKA, i.e., the distances up to a common rescaling, followed by the observable of the Pearson correlation, i.e., the distances up to a positive affine map. Both determine the order of all distances, below which the lattice splits into two branches, with neighbor sets on one branch and spanning trees on the other.

The rankings around every sample leave the minimum spanning tree undetermined, and the tree leaves the neighbor sets undetermined. The two branches share the nearest-neighbor map, which sends each sample to its nearest neighbor and lies below both branches.

Scale and Protocol

The neighbor count \(k\) and the number \(m\) of samples set the scale of a criterion, i.e., the range of distances over which the two models are compared. A criterion also includes its protocol, i.e., the preprocessing of features, the aggregation of scores over layers, and the calibration against randomly paired samples.

03Four Studies on One Lattice

Conflicting Conclusions Can Come from Criteria Applied at Different Points of the Lattice

The four studies differ in the scale, the observable, and the protocol of their criteria, and thereby apply criteria at different points of the lattice. For each study, we place its criteria on the lattice and explain how the positions of these criteria relate to its conclusion.

PRHPlatonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al., 2024)

Agreement on All Distances Is Claimed, and Neighbor Overlap Is Measured

The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) states that networks trained with different objectives, data, and modalities converge toward a shared statistical model of reality. This claim amounts to agreement under the criterion at the top of the lattice, where the idealized learners of PRH agree on all distances.

The evidence of PRH comes from mKNN, a criterion near the bottom that compares neighbor sets, so one pair of models can satisfy an mKNN criterion and fail a criterion on all distances.

ARHAristotelian Representation Hypothesis (Gröger et al., 2026)

The Loss of the CKA Trend Can Come from Calibrating a Maximum over Layers

After calibrating scores against randomly paired samples, ARH finds that CKA largely loses the size trend kept by mKNN and narrows the claimed convergence to shared local neighborhoods. PRH and ARH thus both report the size trend of mKNN, whose observable lies below the order of all distances, and reach different conclusions about agreement above this order.

The calibration threshold of the maximum over layers used by ARH follows the largest random-pairing baseline over layer pairs, which grows with effective dimension for CKA and stays the same for mKNN. When this baseline grows faster than the layer maximum along a model family, calibration can reverse the size trend on unchanged representations, and the loss of the CKA trend in ARH can come from the protocol.

BPCBack into Plato’s Cave (Koepke et al., 2026)

Neighbor Overlap Is Determined by Boundary Crossings at the Scale \(k/m\)

BPC tests the neighbor evidence of PRH on larger evaluation sets and concludes that the shared structure is coarse, since mKNN at a fixed \(k\) collapses unless \(k\) grows in proportion to the number of samples.

At each sample, the neighbor boundary separates the \(k\) nearest neighbors in model A from the other samples, and the loss of neighbor overlap counts the neighbors pushed across this boundary by model B. At a fixed \(k\), this boundary moves closer to each sample on a larger evaluation set, and the collapse of mKNN reported by BPC is therefore at least partly a scale effect. PRH and BPC reach different conclusions from neighbor criteria at different scales.

SOGStructure over Geometry (You et al., 2026)

Spanning Trees Contain the Nearest-neighbor Forest

SOG challenges the local view of ARH with the size trend of the overlap of minimum spanning trees (MSTs), and finds relational structure converging locally in neighbor sets and globally in MSTs.

Every MST contains the nearest-neighbor forest, which joins each sample to its nearest neighbor and makes up at least half of each tree. The part of the MST trend carried by the forest repeats the trend of one-nearest-neighbor overlap. The MST trend therefore supports global convergence to the extent that the bridges between the trees of the forest carry this trend.

Whether a criterion accepts one pair of fixed representations can change with its scale and its observable, and the protocol of a criterion alone can reverse a size trend along a model family.

The conflicting conclusions of the four studies can therefore come from criteria applied at different points of the lattice.

04What Determines the Criteria Met Reliably across Runs

The Training Procedure Decides the Criteria Met Reliably in a Linear Autoencoder, and the Objective in a Finite Contrastive Model

The criteria above compare two fixed representations, whereas repeated training can change the representation of a model. Studies also claim that networks trained with different objectives learn similar representations, so it remains to explore which criteria can be satisfied by a pair of independently trained models.

Agreement under a criterion is reliable if two independently trained runs meet the criterion with probability at least \(1-\delta\). In two theoretical models with explicit minimizers, a linear autoencoder and a finite contrastive model, we show how the training procedure and the objective decide the set of criteria met reliably across runs.

Training procedureTheorem 2, linear autoencoder

The Training Procedure Selects the Gauge of a Linear Head

A model that predicts through a linear head computes the same outputs after an invertible change of its representation compensated in the head, so the task and any amount of its data fix the representation up to such changes. In a linear autoencoder trained by gradient flow, the initialization rather than the data selects this change, called the gauge.

Two such runs then disagree in geometry at every sample size, whereas weight decay or tied weights fix one gauge and remove this disagreement. For large samples in this model, the training procedure rather than the sample size decides whether two runs reliably meet CKA criteria of small tolerance.

ObjectiveTheorem 3, finite Gibbs model

The Objective Decides Whether Contrastive Learners Agree beyond the Order of Distances

Learners trained with different objectives can have unique yet different limits, and two such learners can learn one relation through different increasing functions. We prove that this pattern holds for two contrastive objectives in the finite Gibbs model, a finite contrastive model whose population limit is the idealized contrastive learner of PRH.

As both samples grow, an InfoNCE learner and a spectral contrastive learner reliably agree on the order of all distances and on every observable below this order, whereas CKA between them tends to a limit below one. For large samples in this model, the objective rather than the sample size decides whether two learners reliably meet criteria on scale-normalized geometry with a small tolerance.

Together, the two theorems support the training part of the LRH in the linear autoencoder and in the finite Gibbs model.

05Experiments

We Test Our Explanation on Dozens of Pretrained Models and the Two Theorems on Thousands of Runs

On dozens of pretrained vision and language models, we test whether the scale, the observable, and the protocol of the criteria applied by the four studies account for their conflicting conclusions. Under controlled training, we test the predictions of the two theorems on the gauge selected by the training procedure and on the monotone link set by the objective.

We call an experiment preregistered if we fixed its predictions, evaluation settings, and decision rules before the first run and recorded the hash and time of this plan.

Neighbor Overlap at a Fixed \(k\) Stays Flat or Falls with the Evaluation Size According to the Model Pair

Figure in the BPC card

To test whether a neighbor criterion at a fixed \(k\) compares two models at a finer scale on a larger evaluation set, we measure mKNN on nested COCO evaluation sets of 256 to 16,384 images.

  • At \(k = 10\), the median mKNN of the vision–language pairs falls from 0.091 to 0.0085 on the COCO evaluation sets.
  • The slope of \(\ln s_N\) on \(\ln m\) is set by the model pair, with medians of −0.57 for vision–language pairs, −0.21 for pairs of vision models, and −0.01 for pairs of Pythia-410M models, against −1.00 for chance.
  • At a shared \(k/m\), the medians for \(k = 100\) lie within 4% of those for \(k = 10\), whereas those for \(k = 1\) differ by up to 19%.

Insight 1Our results suggest that the evaluation size sets the scale of mKNN and that scores at a shared \(k/m\) are comparable across evaluation sets.

Shared Spanning-tree Edges Are Mostly Nearest-neighbor Edges, and Pretrained Vision and Language Representations Differ by More than a Monotone Link

Since every MST contains the nearest-neighbor forest, we count the shared MST edges lying in this forest and the shared bridges between its trees on the 136 pairs of a vision model and a language model. A monotone link is one increasing function applied to all distances, and the residual is the difference left around this link. To see whether pretrained vision and language representations differ mostly by such a link or by the residual, we fit increasingly flexible maps between their squared distances. We also ask whether a size trend at a fixed depth already depends on the preprocessing of the features.

  • On an evaluation set of \(m = 2047\) WIT samples, the nearest-neighbor forest makes up a median 0.875 and 0.889 of the shared MST edges at mid depth and the last layer.
  • The number of shared bridges exceeds its chance level in 93% and 100% of pairs at the two depths, and the size trend of this number along BLOOMZ at the last layer stays unresolved.
  • Beyond an affine fit, a global monotone fit explains a further median 0.0004 and 0.0012 of the squared-distance discrepancy, below the preregistered level of 0.10 in all 136 pairs.
  • At mid depth and after subtraction of their mean under random pairing, the BLOOMZ trends of CKA and mKNN are 0.70 and 0.84 with the preprocessing of PRH, and they reverse to −1.00 and −0.92 under plain \(\ell_2\) normalization.

Insight 2Our results suggest that on these pretrained pairs, spanning-tree overlap comes mostly from the nearest-neighbor forest and the deficit \(1-\mathrm{CKA}\) mostly from a residual, rather than from a monotone link.

The Threshold of a Calibrated Layer Maximum Can Be Forecast from Single-model Features

Figure in the ARH card

Along four language-model families, we test whether the calibration threshold of a layer maximum follows the largest random-pairing baseline. We then compare the change of the CKA trend under calibration with the growth of this baseline relative to the raw score.

  • In all four families, the threshold of the layer maximum follows the largest random-pairing baseline, in a post hoc analysis on BLOOMZ and as forecast from single-model features on LLaMA-1, OpenLLaMA, and Pythia.
  • The CKA trend is lost or reversed along BLOOMZ (0.89 to −0.02), LLaMA-1 (0.68 to −0.85), and OpenLLaMA (0.88 to 0.18), where this baseline grows at least as fast as the raw score.
  • The trend is kept along Pythia (0.93 to 0.89), where the raw score grows faster, by 0.024 per doubling of parameters against 0.0063 for the threshold.
  • Given the raw scores, the thresholds forecast from features nevertheless yield a predicted order after calibration in 30 of the 34 comparisons involving OpenLLaMA 13B and in 352 of the 357 Pythia comparisons, and every predicted order is correct.
  • In every family, the trends of mKNN and debiased CKA move by 0.06 or less under calibration, and the CKA trend at mid depth keeps its sign.

Insight 3Our results suggest that the calibration threshold of a layer maximum follows the largest random-pairing baseline. Along the four families, calibration kept the CKA trend where the raw score outgrew this baseline and removed or reversed the trend elsewhere.

The Training Procedure Decides Whether Two Runs of a Linear Autoencoder Agree on Distances

Figure in the Theorem 2 card

We test the predictions of Theorem 2 in a preregistered experiment on the linear autoencoder, at training sample sizes \(N\) from \(2^8\) to the population limit.

  • Under gradient descent without a regularizer, the criteria on neighbor sets, mean-normalized squared distances (\(G_s\)), and CKA each accepted 0 of 48 pairs of runs at every finite \(N\) and 0 of 500 at \(N = \infty\).
  • With weight decay, tied weights, or small initialization, agreement under these criteria was reliable for \(N \ge 2^{12}\).
  • Outside the preregistered tests, the median condition number of the learned \(EE^\top\), with \(E\) the encoder, is 4.66 under a small step close to gradient flow, against 4.67 predicted, and 1.0330 under weight decay, against 1.0329 predicted.

Insight 4Our results suggest that weight decay, tied weights, and small initialization each fix one gauge of the linear autoencoder, which confirms the preregistered predictions of Theorem 2 for these three training procedures.

Agreement of InfoNCE and Spectral Learners on the Order of Distances Becomes Reliable in the Finite Gibbs Model

Figure in the Theorem 3 card

In a preregistered experiment, we compute certified minimizers of the empirical InfoNCE and spectral contrastive risks in the finite Gibbs model. This experiment tests Theorem 3 and our analysis pipeline inside the premises of the theorem, not trained networks.

  • Exact agreement across objectives on the order of all distances among eight evaluation samples held in 0 of 96 pairs up to 228 examples and in 94 of 96 at 234.
  • The size 234 lies near the value \(N^* = 2^{33.75}\) predicted before the runs for agreement in 90% of pairs.
  • At 240 examples, the largest difference of mean-normalized distances across objectives settled at a median 0.348, the limit given by the theorem.
  • After Holm correction, all nine preregistered confirmatory predictions held.

Insight 5Our results suggest that in the finite Gibbs model, certified minimizers of InfoNCE and of the spectral contrastive loss come to agree exactly on the order of distances and differ in geometry by the link. Both findings confirm the preregistered predictions of Theorem 3 inside its premises.

06Reference

Measures and Terms

Alignment criterion

A test that compares how two models represent a shared set of evaluation samples, through one relation among these samples. The criterion accepts the pair of models if the two relations agree within a tolerance under a fixed scale and protocol.

Observable

A map from the pairwise inner products of the samples in one model to one relation among the samples, such as all distances, their order, the neighbor sets, or the minimum spanning tree. One observable refines another if its value determines the value of the other.

mKNN

Mutual \(k\)-nearest-neighbor overlap, i.e., the fraction of the \(k\) nearest neighbors of each sample shared by the two models, averaged over the samples, \(s_N=(mk)^{-1}\sum_i|\mathcal N_k^A(i)\cap\mathcal N_k^B(i)|\). At \(k = 1\) this score is the one-nearest-neighbor overlap, and under a random pairing of the samples its expected value is \(k/(m-1)\).

MST overlap

The fraction of the \(m-1\) edges of the minimum spanning tree (MST) shared by the two models, \(s_{\mathcal T}=|\mathcal T_A\cap\mathcal T_B|/(m-1)\), where each tree joins all samples through edges of smallest total length.

CKA

Linear centered kernel alignment, i.e., the cosine similarity of the two centered Gram matrices of the samples, \(\mathrm{CKA}(K_A,K_B)=\langle\bar K_A,\bar K_B\rangle_F/(\lVert\bar K_A\rVert_F\lVert\bar K_B\rVert_F)\). CKA equals one exactly when the two centered matrices are positive multiples of each other and depends on the distances up to a common rescaling.

Calibration

A correction of a score by its values after a random permutation of the samples of one model. ARH maps an observed score to \(s_{\rm cal}=[s_{\rm obs}-c_\alpha]_+/(s_{\max}-c_\alpha)\), where \(c_\alpha\) is a high quantile of the scores on the observed and the permuted pairings.

Random-pairing baseline

The expected value of a score under a random permutation of the samples of one model, i.e., its chance level. This baseline is \(k/(m-1)\) for mKNN at every layer pair and grows with the effective dimension of each layer for CKA.

Size trend

The mean over vision models of the Spearman correlation between a score and the size of the language model.

Reliable agreement

Agreement under a criterion is reliable if two independently trained runs meet the criterion with probability at least \(1-\delta\). In our experiments, agreement is reliable if the one-sided 95% Clopper–Pearson lower bound on the fraction of pairs of runs within the tolerance exceeds 0.9.

AbstractShow

The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH) holds that increasingly capable models converge toward a common representation. However, recent studies have shown different views on aligning representations via their neighborhoods, spanning trees, and distances. We show that these views differ because the alignment criteria behind them sit at different points of one lattice, and we formalize this explanation as the Lattice Representation Hypothesis (LRH). Under the LRH, the convergence claimed by the PRH is agreement at the top of the lattice, whereas its evidence comes from neighbor overlap, a criterion near the bottom. The Aristotelian Representation Hypothesis (ARH) restricts the claimed convergence to local neighborhoods because calibration keeps the growth of neighbor overlap with model size but largely removes that of centered kernel alignment. We show that this removal can come from calibrating a maximum over layers, so global convergence remains open. Back into Plato’s Cave (BPC) infers coarse shared structure from the collapse of neighbor overlap on larger evaluation sets, whereas we trace this collapse at least partly to a fixed number of neighbors covering shorter distances. We verify the spanning-tree overlap reported by Structure over Geometry (SOG) and show that it largely repeats one-nearest-neighbor overlap. The LRH also extends to independently trained models, whose training procedure and objective decide the criteria met reliably across runs. We prove this claim for the training procedure in a linear autoencoder and for the objective in a finite contrastive Gibbs model. Experiments on dozens of pretrained vision and language models and on thousands of runs of both theoretical models support these conclusions. We hope this work paves the way toward a general theory of representation hypotheses, with a framework for analyzing these hypotheses and comparing their evidence.
